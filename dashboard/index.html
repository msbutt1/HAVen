    Daily vibration limits

    VibeGuard compares each worker’s daily hand-arm vibration with the limits used in European and UK law. This page explains the numbers, where they come from, and what our wrist sensor can and cannot tell you.

    Action value

    2.5 m/s² A(8)

    100 points

    Above this, the employer must act to reduce exposure: rotate jobs, use lower-vibration tools, plan breaks, and offer health surveillance.

    Limit value

    5.0 m/s² A(8)

    400 points

    Must not be exceeded on any day. A supervisor or occupational health can set a lower personal limit (for example 100 points after symptoms). Current personal limit: 400 points.

    Why these limits

    The values come from EU Directive 2002/44/EC, set on the basis of ISO 5349-1 and epidemiological evidence on vibration white finger and other hand-arm vibration effects. The UK adopted them in the Control of Vibration at Work Regulations 2005, and HSE added the exposure points system used here. ACGIH uses similar values, which Canadian provinces often refer to. Alberta has no numeric hand-arm vibration limit (employers have a general duty to control the hazard), so we follow the international standard.

    How we calculate it

    1. Vibration magnitude ahv = √(ax² + ay² + az²), from the wrist accelerometer after removing gravity and slow arm movement.
    2. Only tool time counts. A second is added only while the tool detector says a tool is vibrating and the worker is gripping. If grip is unknown the second still counts, so missing data never hides exposure.
    3. Daily exposure A(8) = √( Σ ahv² × time ÷ 8 h ). Harm scales with vibration² × time, so several tools in one day add up.
    4. Points = 2 × ahv² × hours (HSE). 100 points = action value, 400 = limit value.
    5. Time left at the current intensity = (level − points so far) ÷ points per hour now.

    Worked example: a grinder at 6 m/s² gives 2 × 6² = 72 points per hour. The action value (100) is reached after about 1 h 23 min, the limit (400) after about 5 h 33 min.

    What grip adds

    Grip (forearm EMG) does not multiply the number. It decides whether a second counts as hand–tool contact, and it is reported alongside the dose, as ISO 5349-1 §4.3 asks for coupling forces. We also show how hard the worker grips during contact: the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of effort. Jonsson’s 1982 reference levels are static under 2–5 %, median under 10–14 % and peak under 50–70 % of maximum; they were developed mainly for shoulder and neck muscles, so they are a reference only.

    Long-term risk

    ISO 5349-1 Annex C estimates the years of daily exposure after which 10 % of a group of workers would develop vibration white finger: Dy = 31.8 × A(8)−1.06 years (A(8) 2.5 → about 12 years; 3.0 → 9.9; 5.0 → 5.8). It is a group estimate, not a diagnosis. Later research suggests it is conservative, and we do not show it for A(8) below 1 m/s².

    Where we measure

    HSE’s guidance says measurements away from the palm, including at the wrist, are unlikely to be representative, and that monitoring systems must not be confused with measurement systems (monitors can differ by up to 20 % on the same task). ISO/TS 22270:2026 now gives guidance for monitoring daily exposure with sensors on the hand, wrist or arm, as a supplement to ISO 5349-1. VibeGuard is a monitoring tool: live alerts, trends, grip and tool comparisons. Formal risk assessments still use an ISO 5349-2 measurement or tool data. Next step: a per-tool wrist-to-hand calibration against a reference measurement.

    Limitations

    • A hobby IMU at the wrist (500 Hz, about 5–218 Hz usable band; the ISO band reaches about 1250 Hz). Our A(8) is an estimate.
    • Unweighted by default: compared with ISO Wh weighting this over-states high-frequency tools, while the wrist position tends to under-read the hand.
    • Below the action value is not risk-free. The limits are group-based and individuals differ, which is why a personal limit exists.
    • Not a medical device and not a diagnosis.
